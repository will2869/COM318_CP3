The problem is not satellites. The problem is unmanaged debris.

Space debris is the growing cloud of disabled satellites, rocket bodies, paint flecks, and other fragments orbiting Earth. These objects can collide, break apart, and create even more fragments, making future missions harder and more expensive.

1.2M+ estimated objects larger than 1 cm
40,000 tracked objects in orbit

Three sides of the debate

Innovation first

One viewpoint emphasizes the benefits of large satellite constellations and argues that strict limits could slow innovation, reduce access, and raise costs.

Safety first

A second viewpoint argues that the current launch pace is making orbital safety harder to manage, so stronger enforcement is needed.

Fairness first

A third viewpoint focuses on who should pay, who should set standards, and whether a “polluter pays” model can realistically work in space.

Our position

Satellite growth should continue, but expansion must be paired with predictable funding for active debris removal and long-term orbital cleanup. Best practices alone are not enough if they do not fund real remediation.

  • Fund debris remediation research and development.
  • Support demonstration missions for active debris removal.
  • Create incentives for private companies and nations to participate.
  • Make cleanup funding enforceable, not optional.

Why this matters to young engineers and technology users

Everyone, especially college students and early-career professionals in technology and engineering, are both users and future designers of space infrastructure. Communications, mapping, weather data, navigation, and research all depend on a safe orbital environment.

This campaign frames orbital cleanup as pro-innovation: protecting a shared resource so space technology can keep advancing.
